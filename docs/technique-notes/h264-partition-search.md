# H.264 partition-search reuse

This note answers the five questions in CoreAV's [committed partition-search design](https://github.com/coreav-dev/coreav/blob/c0cbf8c6af39b96ac762bf91f79f0bae10bc5a7b/docs/design/h264-encoder-p.md#partition-search-cost-design). That design predates this study. The earlier [encoder search-efficiency note](h264-encoder-speed.md) explains staged motion scoring, interpolation, incremental evaluation, and heuristic pruning. This note concentrates on the remaining exactness and reuse questions rather than repeating that overview.

The committed profile assigns 39.04% of sampled process cycles to cheap motion search, 37.46% to full rate-distortion optimization finalists, and 10.49% to unchanged-region preparation. These are disjoint stages, not independent optimization opportunities. Interpolation reuse can affect both probes and finalists, and retained base state can affect preparation and finalist work. The linked profile records the workload, host, tools, and sample limitations. This study ran no benchmarks and establishes no per-technique speedup. In particular, a sampled share is not the fraction that an optimization can remove.

## Sources and specification

The first source is [x264 core 165](https://code.videolan.org/videolan/x264/-/tree/0480cb05fa188d37ae87e8f4fd8f1aea3711f7ee), commit `0480cb05fa188d37ae87e8f4fd8f1aea3711f7ee`, also pinned by `scripts/oracle-ffmpeg.env` and the earlier note. x264 has no release tags. This study uses that exact revision, not a moving stable branch. The studied source headers specify GPL-2.0-or-later and also offer commercial licensing. The second source is [OpenH264 v2.7.0](https://github.com/cisco/openh264/tree/1bca62311e447fd9c39fc93de2c4b96cd5ce971c), commit `1bca62311e447fd9c39fc93de2c4b96cd5ce971c`, under BSD-2-Clause. All source references below mean those pins and licences. Both clones were outside the CoreAV repository and were deleted after study.

Rec. ITU-T H.264, version 15, August 2024, is the authority. Clause 8.4.1 governs motion-vector derivation, clause 8.4.2 governs inter sample prediction, clause 8.5 governs inverse scaling and reconstruction, and clauses 7 and 9 govern syntax and entropy coding. Encoder search is not a normative decoder process. A legal but differently chosen candidate can still violate CoreAV's byte-identical comparison contract. Annex A admission, including motion-count and macroblock-bit limits, remains separate from cheap ranking.

## Interpolation reuse without picture-wide preparation

Question 4 has the largest identified cheap-search component, fractional prediction at 14.48 percentage points of process cycles. The useful distinction is between retaining filtered reference samples and retaining a candidate's completed prediction. The former serves overlapping probes. The latter can avoid predicting the same finalist again, addressing some of the separate 4.32-point changed-region prediction cost. Neither saving requires a different motion-search policy.

x264's `common/mc.c`, in `x264_frame_filter` and `get_ref`, supplies persistent half-sample reference planes and returns a reference view when a phase needs no new averaging or weighting. Its `encoder/me.c`, in `refine_subpel`, also obtains extended regions whose overlapping views serve neighbouring candidate comparisons. OpenH264's `codec/encoder/core/src/md.cpp`, in `MeRefineFracPixel`, instead prepares local filtered scratch shared by nearby fractional probes and copies the selected prediction into macroblock prediction storage. Its `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_base_layer_md.cpp`, in `WelsMdInterMbRefinement` and `WelsMdInterEncode`, retains prediction work across refinement and encoding. These observations distinguish reference-sample reuse, overlapping probe reuse, and selected-prediction retention. They do not establish a general cache shared by every search and finalist.

Repeated access to overlapping interpolation support follows from the problem. Full-picture filtered planes, aligned scratch strides, and pointer views are choices made by these C and C++ kernel interfaces. x264's batch pixel comparisons favor compatible strides. OpenH264 shows that local scratch is also practical, but its selected-prediction lifetime is narrower than CoreAV's proposed cross-probe and finalist reuse. A design without those interfaces can retain checked read-only regions and completed predictions separately, choosing each lifetime by actual reuse rather than adopting either buffer layout.

CoreAV's committed bounded local window remains the first comparison arm. The studied code supports testing overlap reuse within that window, but does not justify paying for full-picture planes. Retaining predictions only for ranked finalists is a distinct alternative when full phase preparation would mostly be unused. The comparison needs preparation work and retained storage as well as consumed predictions and full encode time. A cache hit that still copies or recomputes the entire prediction may save less than its name suggests.

The correctness boundary remains clause 8.4.2.2.1 for luma and clause 8.4.2.2.2 for chroma. Edge extension, negative coordinates, intermediate precision, rounding, and clipping must agree with the existing predictor. The reference identity, position, extent, component, and vector determine whether a retained prediction is reusable. Reference replacement invalidates it. CoreAV's sampled fallback for outlying predictors avoids turning an ordinary local-window optimization into a restriction on legal vectors. Both implementations motivate reuse, but neither proves that a particular local cache beats this committed design.

## Candidate identity without a full visited-array scan

Question 1 addresses 8.53 percentage points of process cycles in visited-candidate lookup. A dense list can avoid inspecting unused capacity without changing any scored candidate or tie. That is different from removing a candidate because a similar predictor was already considered. Exact memoization must return the same score under the same scoring inputs, not merely recognize a familiar vector.

x264's `common/common.h`, in the scalar `x264_predictor_clip` and `x264_predictor_roundclip`, compacts accepted predictor candidates and returns their count. These helpers exclude zero and the designated predictor before clipping. They do not establish pairwise uniqueness of the retained candidates, including candidates that become equal after clipping. `encoder/me.c`, in `x264_me_search_ref`, consumes the counted list and can reuse a known predictor cost when its precision already matches the integer probe. OpenH264's `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_motion_estimate.cpp`, in `WelsMotionEstimateInitialPoint`, iterates a counted predictor list and avoids scoring a candidate equal to the current best rounded and clipped vector. That is not a complete visited-score cache either.

The problem supplies the reason to avoid duplicate expensive scoring. It does not require a global visited set. These paths manage a small predictor population and a current best result, whereas CoreAV also retains a ranking and stage centres. The studied raw arrays and compact vector representations suit their kernel interfaces. They provide no evidence that hashing or a coordinate table would improve CoreAV's bounded scalar search. A design free of those representations can distinguish initialized entries from capacity and keep exact-score validity explicit.

CoreAV's dense-prefix proposal directly removes the unused-entry traversal and preserves its existing ranking, protected seeds, and tuple tie order. It is a closer fit than either source's limited duplicate avoidance. A coordinate-indexed alternative must repay its initialization and handle valid predictors outside the ordinary window. It must also preserve candidate identity after any coordinate conversion. Clauses 8.4.1 and 7.4.5.1 still govern admitted vectors and differences. Caching a motion score beyond one fixed scoring context would additionally require matching the predictor, reference, block, and metric. Agreement of vector coordinates alone does not imply equal motion-difference cost.

## Exact score bounds are stricter than search termination

Question 2 covers the 2.88-point motion-difference counting cost and part of the 13.15-point SAD and remaining cheap-search work. Adding exact motion-signalling cost before nonnegative pixel error can reject an uncompetitive cheap score sooner. Direct signed Exp-Golomb length derivation under clause 9.1 avoids exercising a general bit writer for each probe. SAD, the sum of absolute sample differences, can accumulate a lower bound on that same cheap objective. It is not a bound on complete reconstruction distortion plus encoded rate.

Both sources avoid general bitstream emission in motion scoring. x264's `encoder/me.c`, in `x264_me_search_ref` and `refine_subpel`, uses predictor-relative motion-cost lookups. OpenH264's `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_motion_estimate.cpp`, in `WelsMotionEstimateInitialPoint` and `WelsDiamondSearch`, also adds lookup-based motion costs to pixel comparisons. Their tables are evidence that signalling-cost setup can leave the hot scoring path, not an arithmetic definition for CoreAV to import.

There is a related lower-bound use in x264's `refine_subpel` cost evaluation: a luma-plus-motion score can avoid further nonnegative chroma comparison work when the candidate cannot improve the current result. The studied OpenH264 initial-point path instead includes a predicted-cost search stop, and its diamond path can stop on lack of improvement. Neither path demonstrates CoreAV's row-wise cutoff with multiple ranked candidates and independent stage centres. A predicted-cost stop omits search work heuristically. It is not proof that an omitted candidate could not affect CoreAV's result.

Nonnegative accumulation is mathematical, not a C or SIMD constraint. The sources' full-block and batched comparison interfaces favor regular work, while a scalar evaluator can benefit from row-level abandonment. A design free of those interfaces can expose a bound to the scorer without changing the candidate generator. Interpolation reuse complicates the payoff: abandoning SAD late may save little if all filtered samples and the completed prediction were already prepared. The experiment must measure the combined work, not only the number of skipped pixel comparisons.

CoreAV's committed design is more exacting than the observed single-best comparisons. A rejected probe must be unable to change either the retained ranking or its active search centre. Integer-centre decisions can remain relevant even when fractional seeds already occupy the ranking. Incomplete rankings and protected seeds also prevent some cutoffs. Strict excess preserves equal-cost ties. An abandoned score is a rejection certificate for particular consumers, not an exact cached value. The uncached comparison must cover revisits and later consumers that need a tighter bound or a complete score. The study supplies no reason to weaken any of those conditions or replace direct Exp-Golomb arithmetic with either implementation's tables.

## Retained base work must respect chroma and syntax coupling

Question 3 spans the 10.49-point preparation stage and substantial finalist work. Its central opportunity is to preserve already valid base-block results when the preceding partition wins. Reconstructing the unchanged region separately for each searched partition repeats work whose inputs may be identical. The benefit depends on preserving the actual motion map, not recognizing a familiar partition-layout name.

The source comparison reveals an important limit on local rate-distortion evidence. x264's `encoder/rdo.c`, in `rd_cost_mb`, evaluates a complete encoded macroblock, whereas `x264_rd_cost_part` and `rd_cost_subpart` also use region-focused evaluation. In `encoder/macroblock.c`, `macroblock_encode_p8x8_internal` omits chroma DC from its local subsampled-chroma residual reconstruction. The smaller `macroblock_encode_p4x4_internal` path is luma-only for 4:2:0. In contrast, `mb_encode_chroma_internal` processes the coupled chroma DC for complete chroma encoding. Consequently, the existence of fast local x264 costs is not evidence that they equal CoreAV's complete candidate objective. Omitting that coupling would be an objective change, not exact incremental evaluation.

OpenH264 supplies the complementary boundary. `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_base_layer_md.cpp`, in `WelsMdInterMbRefinement` and `WelsMdInterEncode`, passes prepared predictions into encoding rather than proving a full syntax-counted finalist cache. `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_encode_mb.cpp`, in `WelsEncRecUV`, processes chroma DC jointly and handles AC separately. This supports separating reusable prediction and transform inputs from coupled reconstruction. It does not demonstrate that its mode-decision path maintains CoreAV's exact finalist contract.

The distinction follows from clause 8.5's reconstruction process. A changed chroma block can change the jointly transformed DC values and reconstructed samples outside that block. Retaining unchanged AC levels therefore does not make unchanged chroma reconstruction independent. Clause 8.4.1 adds motion-predictor dependencies, and clause 9.2 adds CAVLC coefficient-neighbour dependencies. Coded-block patterns and candidate admission also need complete information. CoreAV's committed design correctly retains independent block work while recomputing coupled chroma DC and every reconstruction it affects.

The 8.65-point complete-syntax-and-cost share deserves a separate caution. x264's `encoder/rdo.c` obtains CAVLC counting from the syntax writer without emitting the stream. Its CABAC estimation uses a different cost model and temporary contexts. The earlier note covers that distinction. Shared syntax traversal prevents count and emission from drifting, but the traversal still costs work. Neither studied encoder establishes a reusable, exact additive rate for arbitrary CoreAV partition replacements. Retaining the complete syntax walk initially is safer than treating the measured share as an invitation to cache isolated bit totals.

Mutable macroblock contexts, scratch copies, and macro-specialized writers follow the studied implementations' interfaces rather than H.264 itself. A design free of those constraints can keep a committed local base and private candidate state, with validity tied to the inputs of retained results. That is already CoreAV's committed alternative. Selection advances the base, rejection leaves it intact, and layout transitions revalidate retained blocks against the new map, including unsearched regions. Retained finalist prediction can be reused only when all its prediction inputs match. Equality against the complete evaluator must include candidate keys, levels, reconstruction, costs, refusals, and rollback. A fast region cost with a narrower objective cannot serve as that equality oracle.

## Layout omission remains a separate quality policy

Question 5 is a possible reduction in all the preceding work, but it is not part of the byte-identical arm. x264's `encoder/analyse.c`, in `mb_analyse_inter_p16x8`, `mb_analyse_p_rd`, and `x264_macroblock_analyse`, gates some partition analysis and RDO using cheap costs and analysis settings. OpenH264's `codec/encoder/core/src/svc_base_layer_md.cpp`, in `WelsMdInterFinePartition`, also makes some additional layout searches conditional on cheaper partition results. The earlier note explains the wider pruning and skip tradeoff. These paths do not prove that the omitted layouts lose the complete objective.

Clauses 7.3.5 and 8.4 permit legal partition layouts without requiring exhaustive encoder search. Coherent motion makes larger partitions plausible, which follows from the problem. The encoders' particular thresholds, mode ordering, and configurable analysis policies reflect their quality and latency choices. A design without those constraints can keep the legal candidate set separate from a reversible search policy instead of adopting either control flow.

CoreAV's committed answer remains appropriate: keep the exact-skip rule, layout order, greedy subgroup choices, motion budget, and finalist caps while testing exact reuse. A later whole-block-plus-best-split comparison can reduce layout count, but its omitted searches and complete output must be recorded alongside time, bitrate, PSNR, and SSIM on identical prepared pictures. Equal numeric QP does not establish equal quality or equal rate. Neither the sampled profile nor this source reading selects that policy in advance.

## Study boundary

The sources support experiments, not adoption. Each exact arm must first match the uncached evaluator and complete bitstream and reconstruction, including deterministic ties and transactional failure. It then needs a same-run comparison against the committed design or another independently derived alternative. Heuristic arms additionally need rate and quality comparisons. Conformance and the encoder similarity guard remain unchanged.

This note contains no production code. This study context and its independent note reviewer must never implement the H.264 encoder. Only a fresh implementation context may use the specification and the independently reviewed, committed note, without opening the studied sources.
